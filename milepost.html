Two states, two conveners, one delivery footprint. New Mexico with HealthInno. Oregon with Rainshadow Health Innovation.

Most rural pilots never find a rural site.

A company gets a warm meeting with a critical access hospital, then waits nine months for a clinic with four staff to find the hours. The pilot dies in scheduling, not in science.

We are the site

Waystation holds the contracts and employs the promotoras and nurse care managers behind them. A Milepost pilot runs inside work that is already happening, on a panel that is already enrolled, so nobody has to invent capacity to host you.

The population is the hard one

Frontier counties, Spanish first households, no broadband, people on an ordinary telephone. If a product holds up here it holds up anywhere, and almost nothing in digital health has been tested here at all.

The money has a clock on it

New Mexico and Oregon hold $408.8m of first year Rural Health Transformation money between them, and the performance period closes in 2030. State agencies are looking for things to deploy now, not in three budget cycles.

Two states, run two different ways.

In New Mexico the convening already exists and we deliver into it. In Oregon we are standing the convening up.

New Mexico

with HealthInno

New Mexico already has a statewide health innovation convener. HealthInno was incubated by Builders VC and backed by the New Mexico Economic Development Department, and its Health ImpACT Challenge is the state's pilot and learning network, run in collaboration with UNM Health Sciences.

We do not duplicate that. HealthInno sets the statewide priorities, runs the national call and vets the companies. Waystation is the delivery site: the rural counties where a selected product actually meets a patient. A company accepted into Health ImpACT can be routed to a Milepost deployment when the priority it answers is a rural one.

Oregon

with Rainshadow

Oregon has $197.3m of the same federal money, 36 counties, 10 of them frontier, and 1.4 million people living rural. It has no statewide health innovation convener of the kind New Mexico now has.

Rainshadow Health Innovation is that convener, built on the same model: a neutral statewide table where Oregon health systems, coordinated care organizations, tribal health programs and public funders set shared priorities, review vetted technology and host scoped pilots. Its flagship is the Health ACCORD Challenge. Waystation is the rural delivery partner behind it.

How a Milepost runs.

Five markers. Each one has a date, a named owner on both sides and a stop option that nobody has to apologise for taking.

  1. MP 1

    The priority comes first

    Cohorts are opened against a priority a state's own health leaders have named, through HealthInno in New Mexico and through Rainshadow in Oregon. We do not open a cohort because a technology is interesting.

  2. MP 2

    Readiness review, four weeks

    We read for one thing: can this be standing up in a clinic with four staff and a bad connection. Security review, data agreement, workflow fit, and a plain answer on what breaks when the phone has one bar.

  3. MP 3

    Field build, four weeks

    We write the protocol with you, train the promotoras and nurse care managers who will run it, and set the success measure before anyone sees a result. You get the people who will use it in the room while it is still changeable.

  4. MP 4

    Live deployment, eight to twelve weeks

    50 to 200 patients in a defined cohort across two or three counties. Weekly operational readout, a single escalation path, and one clinical owner who can halt it in an hour.

  5. MP 5

    The write up, and a real decision

    A short report with the measure, the operational cost, what the staff said and what we would change. Continue, expand to the next counties, revise, or stop. The report goes back to the state table either way, including when the answer is stop.

Who this is for.

Milepost is a deployment programme, not a demo day. There is no pitch event, no stage and no prize.

A fit if you are

  • Shipping a product that can be in a clinic inside eight weeks
  • Cleared by the FDA, or outside its remit altogether
  • Willing to be measured on something a care team cares about, like follow up rate, time returned to staff or a closed care gap
  • Able to work in Spanish, or willing to get there before MP 4
  • Able to degrade gracefully on a landline, a flip phone or one bar of signal
  • Comfortable being told to stop

Not a fit if you are

  • Pre product, or still building the first version
  • Pursuing FDA clearance and dependent on it to show value
  • Dependent on a clinical trial to demonstrate anything
  • Expecting a purchase order at the end of it
  • Expecting broadband, a smartphone or an in house IT team at the site
  • Looking for a logo and a press release

You do not need to be based in New Mexico or Oregon. Most companies we want are not.

What we look for.

Statewide priorities are set by each state's own table and change as they come in. These are the standing rural problems underneath them, the ones that show up in every county we work in.

Reaching people between visits

Chronic care and behavioral health follow up for people who will not open an app, in households where the nearest clinic is an hour each way. Remote monitoring that survives bad connectivity. Anything that makes a check in happen that would not otherwise have happened.

Moving the panel ratio

A nurse care manager carries about 200 chronic care patients. Everything that safely raises that number is the economics of rural care management. Triage, structured outreach, documentation that writes itself, work that routes to the right person without a meeting.

Billing what is already being done

Rural organisations deliver chronic care management, community health integration and behavioral health integration every day and bill almost none of it. Eligibility, documentation and claim workflows that turn delivered care into collected revenue.

Coordination across organisations that do not share a record

Transitions out of a critical access hospital, referrals into a tribal health programme, a county behavioral health team and a managed care plan all holding one piece. Visibility and routing across organisations rather than inside one.

Workforce that is not there yet

Training, supervision and decision support that let a community health worker do more of what a nurse does today, and a nurse do more of what a physician does today, inside what each is licensed for.
